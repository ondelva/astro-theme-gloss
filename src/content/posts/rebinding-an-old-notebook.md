The spine of the 2016 notebook split along the glue last week, and sixty pages fell onto the floor in a neat stack, like a deck of cards someone had decided to deal.

I picked them up and put them on the desk and looked at them for a while. Then, because I am the sort of person who learns a new skill to avoid admitting a small loss, I looked up how to sew a book.

## The method, briefly

You need a needle, linen thread, a bone folder if you have one and the back of a spoon if you do not, and a little patience. The pages gather into groups of four sheets, called signatures, which are folded and nested. You sew along the fold, one signature to the next, and the thread does the job the glue used to.

I made four signatures out of the loose pages, and by the second one my stitches were even. By the third, I had stopped thinking about it. It is a good job for hands. Sewing is mostly ++arithmetic done slowly++ with thread.

## What I found

I had not looked at the early pages in years. They are a record, in the way a drawer is a record. A phone number. A list of things to bring to a move. A paragraph about a friend who has since stopped writing back, which I did not expect to reread and did, twice.

On page forty-one there is a sentence in my handwriting that I do not remember writing: "Make the thing smaller, then smaller again." No context. No date. I have been trying for years to work out what I meant, and I think the real answer is that it is advice to a person who was me, and who I cannot ask.[^1]

## Where it went wrong

The first signature I sewed backwards. I did not find out until I had done all four and tried to turn the pages, and found that page twelve came after page thirty. I cut the thread and did it again, which took an evening I had not planned to spend.

The second mistake was the glue. I had read that a thin strip of paste along the spine, once the sewing was done, would hold the thing together. I used too much, and it seeped between two pages near the middle and fused them. Pages 88 and 89 are now one slightly stiff page. I looked at what was on them. A shopping list and half a sketch of a chair. I decided I could live with it.

The third mistake was less practical. I had assumed that fixing the book would be a way of preserving it, as if it were a thing in a glass case. It is not. It is a notebook. It has been in bags and on trains and once, memorably, in a bath, and the repair is simply the next thing that has happened to it.

## Why bother

I could have taped the spine, or bought a box and put the loose pages in it, or scanned them and thrown the paper away. Each of those is sensible. I did none of them because none of them would have let me touch every page again, in order, with a needle in my hand.

There is a kind of attention you pay when you are sewing, and it is not the kind you pay when you are reading. You notice the weight of the paper, and how the ink has changed colour. You notice that in March of that year, I pressed harder. I do not know why. The paper remembers it, and I do not.

## The result

The new binding is not neat. The thread shows at the spine, a little loose, and the cover no longer quite matches the pages. It opens flat, which it never did before. I rather like it.

I now know the notebook can be repaired, which was not something I believed. I suspect I will find the same of several other things.[^2]

[^1]: My handwriting in 2016 leaned hard to the right, as if into a wind. It has since straightened up.

[^2]: I have since seen the thread type in a shop and bought four spools. I do not need four spools.
